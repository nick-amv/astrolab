Interest or Ability: Where Career Choice Actually Begins

Two 12-year longitudinal studies by Hoff, Chu, Einarsdóttir and colleagues (Applied Psychology, 2022) — 1775 adolescents in total — found that early interests predicted income and occupational prestige 12 years later. Not grades, not ability scores. Here is what that changes about the logic of choosing a career.

In short

A systematic review of 105 studies (Hoff, Song et al., Journal of Vocational Behavior, 2020, N = 39,602) found interest-occupation fit consistently correlates with job satisfaction (ρ = 0.19) across six decades of data. Interest is the better starting point: it predicts engagement, and engagement builds competence. Abilities are easier to observe from the outside, which is why people look at them first — but that does not mean they should come first.

7 min readMethod & Self-DiscoveryAugust 14, 2026

A teacher gives a high grade in mathematics. Parents file it away. By tenth grade, "you have a mathematical mind" has turned into a career plan — and no one asked whether you actually enjoy doing it. That is how abilities become a trajectory: not because they are the best starting point, but because they are the easiest thing to observe from the outside.

Where the trap comes from

Abilities are visible. You can measure them with grades, class rankings, competition results. Interest cannot be graded. So adults who want to help instinctively reach for what is observable. According to the NCES (2018), roughly 30% of U.S. first-year students change their major within three years — and most of the time not because they changed their minds, but because they chose from the outside in: by grade, by job title, by someone else's advice. What they found inside felt foreign.

This does not mean abilities do not matter. They do. The problem is that they are a poor first question when choosing a career.

What twelve years of observation showed

In 2022, Kevin Hoff, Chu, Einarsdóttir and colleagues published in Applied Psychology the results of two independent longitudinal studies: 485 and 1290 adolescents followed from their teens into their early thirties. In both samples, interests measured during adolescence significantly predicted four career outcomes twelve years later: degree attainment, occupational prestige, job satisfaction — and income.

The last one is the most surprising. Most people assume earnings are driven by competence — skills, experience, results. It turns out early interests play a role too.

Interest has a strange property: it builds the very skill you are told to demonstrate first — through practice no one has to force you into.

Why interest builds ability

Psychologist K. Anders Ericsson showed in his research of the 1990s that mastery accumulates through deliberate practice — intentional, focused, with feedback on errors. Thousands of hours of that kind of practice do not come from discipline alone; they come from the internal drive to return to a problem again and again. Interest creates that drive. What looks like "natural talent" is usually accumulated practice that others did not notice because it looked like a hobby.

That is exactly the chain Hoff et al. trace: interest → choice of direction → sustained practice → mastery → income. The chain starts in adolescence — long before anyone could "prove" any particular ability.

Newport's argument — and why it is not a contradiction

In 2012 Cal Newport, a computer science professor at Georgetown University, published "So Good They Can't Ignore You" with a blunt thesis: do not follow your passion — build mastery. His argument is that most people do not have a ready-made passion waiting to be found; it appears after becoming genuinely good at something.

Newport is pushing back against passive waiting for a "calling" — sit tight until passion arrives. Hoff et al. document something different: even a weak early interest in a direction predicts a better career outcome ten years later than having no interest at all. The gap between "I like figuring out how things work" at sixteen and "nothing much appeals to me" is already meaningful.

The ability trap

When someone follows their abilities without regard for interest, they end up objectively skilled at work that does not draw them in. A systematic review by Hoff, Song and colleagues (Journal of Vocational Behavior, 2020) — 105 studies, nearly 40,000 participants across 65 years — found the link between interest fit and job satisfaction holds in every time period examined. The correlation is modest (ρ = 0.19), but it never disappears. Being good at your work and feeling good in it are different things. The first does not guarantee the second.

What to do

  1. Identify what draws you in — not job titles, but activities: digging into how things work, helping people solve problems, creating forms. That is the raw material.
  2. Take a free career assessment — it translates those inclinations into an interest profile and shows which occupations match most strongly.
  3. Pick 2–3 results and test them by doing something inside the field, not just reading about it. Interest is validated through action.
  4. Do not wait for your abilities to "catch up." You need a baseline — not the ceiling. The ceiling is built by practice, and practice is driven by interest.

Common questions

What matters more when choosing a career — interest or ability?

Hoff et al. (Applied Psychology, 2022, n = 1775) found that adolescent interests predicted income and occupational prestige 12 years later more reliably than grades. Interest is the better starting point. Abilities matter, but most of them are trainable when motivation is sufficient — and motivation comes from interest.

What should I do if I have no clear interests?

Try different activities — do something inside a field rather than just reading about it. Interest often emerges after first contact, not before. A career assessment can surface weak signals that are hard to notice on your own.

If I am good at something I find uninteresting, should I keep doing it?

Short-term, yes — it is a real resource. But 105 studies across 65 years (Hoff, Song et al., Journal of Vocational Behavior, 2020, N = 39,602) show that interest-occupation fit correlates with job satisfaction (ρ = 0.19). Work without interest costs more effort and raises the risk of burnout — even when you are objectively good at it.
