5 Mistakes When Choosing a Career — and Why They're So Hard to Spot

According to NCES (2018), about 30% of first-year U.S. students change their major within three years — not because they changed their minds, but because they chose from the outside in. Here are five patterns that lead there, and why they're so easy to miss at the time.

The short version

The most common mistakes in career choice: picking by prestige rather than by actual tasks, trusting others' opinions over your own interests, confusing what you're good at with what you want to do, making salary the first filter, and waiting for clarity instead of trying things. What links them: looking from the outside in, not from the inside out.

Most of these mistakes don't look like mistakes when you're making them. They look like common sense: "choose something respectable," "listen to people with experience," "think about the money." That's exactly what makes them so persistent. Let's take them one by one.

Mistake 1. Choosing by prestige, not by what the job actually involves

Psychologist Linda Gottfredson described this mechanism in 1981 in her circumscription and compromise theory: by age 13, teenagers have already built an unconscious map of "acceptable careers" — and the filtering happens mostly on two dimensions: gender-appropriateness and prestige. What the work actually consists of — what you'd be doing every day — barely enters the picture.

The outcome is predictable. Someone goes to law school because "lawyer sounds impressive," then realises three years in that the bulk of the job is reviewing contracts in a quiet office — and that isn't them. Or chooses medicine for the status, without weighing what it means to work with critically ill patients every day. Prestige at the moment of choice and satisfaction in the job are weakly connected.

A list of "in-demand careers" describes the market, not you. The match between your profile and the actual tasks of a job is what predicts whether you'll stay in it.

Mistake 2. Trusting others' opinions more than your own interests

Research by Mutlu, Ziegler and Granato, published in Frontiers in Psychology in 2024 (sample of 1,236 students), showed that social approval — from parents, friends, teachers — acts as a filter that removes career options before you've had a chance to think them through. The authors called this "approval-sensitive career guidance": orienting by what others will approve of, rather than by what fits you.

The mechanism is subtle. Nobody says "don't go there" out loud. But when you mention wanting to be a game developer and your father goes quiet, you quietly start choosing from a different list. Or you say "I'm thinking about art school" and read the room — and the hesitation in people's eyes starts to feel like evidence. Outside opinions are useful as a reality check. As an initial filter, they're dangerous.

Mistake 3. Confusing "I'm good at it" with "I want to do it"

This is the mistake most often made by high achievers. Strong in math? Must be engineering. Best writer in class? Must be journalism. The logic is understandable — but it substitutes ability for interest.

A meta-analysis by Rounds and Su in Current Directions in Psychological Science (2014) pulled these constructs apart. Abilities predict how good you'll be at the work. Interests predict how well you'll feel in it — that is, whether you'll still be there in five years. They can align, but often don't. You can be excellent at something that gives you no pleasure at all, and burn out precisely because it "comes naturally."

The counterintuitive finding from Rounds and Su: interests were a more stable predictor of career choice and satisfaction than abilities. We're conditioned to think "being good at it" matters more than "wanting it."

Mistake 4. Starting with salary

Salary is a useful filter — but a terrible first question. When it leads, you choose from a pool of high-ceiling fields without realising that the top of those ranges goes to people who genuinely invested in the work. The upper figure in any salary band isn't paid for tenure; it's paid for mastery, and mastery grows on interest.

Every range also has a floor. Someone without a matching interest profile is most likely to land there — and neither the "earning potential" nor the prestige will fix that. The right order: find the direction where your fit is strongest, then run the numbers.

Mistake 5. Waiting for clarity instead of trying things

"I haven't decided yet" often means "I'm waiting for it to become obvious on its own." It doesn't. Vocational interests aren't a voice from the universe that will eventually speak. They surface through contact with real tasks: a short course, a conversation with someone who does the job, a small project. Without that contact, any choice is theoretical.

The same NCES data (2018) found that 52% of students who'd declared a mathematics major changed track — the highest rate in all of STEM. Not because math is hard. Because it tends to be chosen for its reputation ("math = smart"), with little idea of what a mathematician actually does at work on a Tuesday morning.

What to do

  1. Ask yourself: am I choosing this because I actually enjoy doing something specific — or because it "sounds good"? Those are different answers.
  2. Separate "I'm good at it" from "I want to do it." Make two mental lists and see where they overlap. That's a good place to start.
  3. Find one real person in each field on your shortlist. Don't just google — talk to them. Ask: "What are you actually doing at 11am on a regular Tuesday?"
  4. Take a free career assessment — it maps your interests onto a RIASEC profile and surfaces fields where the match is strongest. It's a starting point, not a verdict.
  5. Don't wait for final clarity. Pick two or three directions and start exploring. Six months from now you'll have far more data than you do today.

Frequently asked questions

What is the most common mistake when choosing a career?

Choosing by prestige rather than by what the job actually involves. Linda Gottfredson showed as early as 1981 that by age 13, teenagers unconsciously filter out careers on two criteria: whether they're gender-appropriate and whether they're prestigious. What the work actually feels like day-to-day barely registers — yet it's precisely what a meta-analysis by Rounds and Su (2014) found to be the most reliable predictor of career satisfaction and persistence.

Why do so many students change their major?

According to NCES (2018), about 30% of U.S. students change their major within the first three years — and in most cases it's not a change of heart. The choice was made based on a job title, parental advice, or status, without a clear sense of what the person actually does in that field on a typical Tuesday.

How do you avoid mistakes when choosing a career?

Start not with "what should I become" but with "what do I actually like doing." Then check: is that an interest or an ability — they're different things. Find one real person in each field on your short list and ask what their 11am looks like on an ordinary workday. And take a structured assessment: an interest profile based on the RIASEC model is a reliable starting point, because the model has been independently tested for its predictive power on both career choice and satisfaction (Nye et al., 2012).
