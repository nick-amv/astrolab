Transferable Skills: What You Take With You When Changing Careers

In 2022 McKinsey studied 77 standard career transition pathways in the United States and found that in 70% of them the primary transferable asset was interpersonal skills — not industry knowledge. That changes the way you should think about yourself, especially when it feels like you're heading into the unknown.

In brief

Changing careers rarely means starting from zero. Nobel laureate James Heckman in Labour Economics (2012) showed that communication, self-discipline, and goal-setting skills reliably predict professional success regardless of industry. The U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET database maps 35 foundational skills across 1,016 occupations — and most of them are not field-specific. Your experience is not a blank slate. It is starting capital.

In 1966 the philosopher Michael Polanyi put his finger on something that turns out to be good news for career changers: "We can know more than we can tell." Tacit knowledge accumulates over years — in the ability to read a room, build a process from scratch, negotiate without winners and losers. It never makes it onto a resume because it is hard to name. And it is exactly what crosses into the next role.

What transferable skills actually are

The label is not jargon. Transferable skills are competencies that work across several occupations without fundamental retraining. They fall into three rough layers.

Universal: written and spoken communication, information analysis, time management, critical thinking. Every field needs them — from accounting to UX design.

Professionally transferable: a construction project manager's skills work equally well in a software team or a marketing agency. A teacher's ability to explain transfers into corporate training, instructional design, or user research.

Technical overlaps: a data analyst moving into marketing keeps Excel and statistical reasoning; a lawyer moving into compliance takes the entire regulatory toolkit.

The U.S. Department of Labor's O*NET database maps 35 foundational skills across 1,016 occupations, organized into seven categories — content, process, social, complex problem solving, technical, systems, and resource management. Most of them are not attached to a specific degree.

Why we underestimate what we already know

Polanyi's 1966 point was that tacit knowledge accumulates as intuition and work habits — things that get done, not named. That is why a career changer often looks at their own experience and sees "the wrong background." They are simply not equipped to view it from the outside.

Heckman and Kautz in Labour Economics (2012) documented the employer side of the same problem: soft skills are hard to measure, so for a long time formal credentials served as a proxy. An imprecise proxy — someone with strong transferable skills can read as an outsider until they learn to present what they actually do.

Because tacit knowledge is invisible from the inside, career changers routinely undervalue themselves relative to how a recruiter from a different industry would assess them.

What transfers best

McKinsey's report on experience-based job progressions (2022) analyzed 77 standard transition pathways across U.S. occupations. In 53 of the 77 — exactly 70% — the decisive transferable asset was interpersonal skills: listening, negotiating, functioning under uncertainty. Technical knowledge came second.

The logic holds. Technical skills are tied to specific tools or processes and need updating when those change. Communication, stress management, and structuring a complex problem — they do not.

What transfers well: project and task management; client- or audience-facing work; training others; analytical thinking; writing and structuring information. What transfers less cleanly: specialized software, industry-specific regulations, technical protocols. Those are precisely the things you can learn — and usually faster than expected, because the mental framework is already there.

How to inventory what you have

One approach: ask not "what can I do?" but "what have I done?" Concrete tasks surface the skills behind them. Negotiated a supplier contract? That is communication, risk management, planning. Onboarded new staff? Information structuring, patience, instructional design.

A second approach: use O*NET OnLine (onetonline.org). The tool shows how closely your current occupation overlaps with the one you are aiming for. The bigger the overlap, the less you need to fill in from scratch. It is not guesswork — it is a map with a known starting point.

How to present this to a new employer

When you arrive from a different field, the hiring manager has one question: what in your background actually works here? The answer requires translation — from the language of your last profession into the language of the new one.

A teacher moving into UX: "I designed learning programs, assessed understanding, and adapted my delivery to each audience" — that is user research and usability testing in a different wrapper. Name it that way, and the perception barrier drops.

Employers are not looking for a diploma. They are looking for someone who will solve a problem. Your job is to show which problems you have already solved and draw a bridge to the new context.

What to do right now

  1. List the last 10 tasks you did at work — not job titles, actual tasks. Write down the skills each one required.
  2. Check the overlap with your target role on O*NET OnLine — you will see where the gap is small and where it is real.
  3. Translate three of your core skills into the language of the new field: show that the same work goes by a different name.
  4. Take the Astrolab assessment — it will show what is strongest in your profile and where that points.

Common questions

Are transferable skills only soft skills?

No. Technical skills transfer too — especially methodical ones: data analysis, project management, information structuring. Soft skills transfer in most cases; technical skills transfer where there is overlap in tools or processes. McKinsey found that in 70% of the 77 career pathways studied, the decisive asset was interpersonal skills — but that does not mean technical skills are irrelevant.

How quickly can you fill the gap in missing skills?

McKinsey estimates that most people transitioning through a related field close the technical gap within 3 to 12 months — provided the foundation of transferable skills is already in place. Without that foundation it takes longer, because you end up learning not just tools but an entire way of working.

Does volunteer experience or a hobby count?

It counts. A skill is a pattern of action, not a line in an employment record. If you have organized a community, coached others, managed a project, or written — that is experience you can present. The task is to translate it into the language of the new field.
